“Managed Agents for Business” describes the category, but customers still need a reason to choose yours. Tasklet already advertises business workflows, approvals and per-agent spending limits. Your ephemeral, tightly permissioned architecture is sensible. It makes the product safer and potentially cheaper to operate, but it is replicable. E2B instances, dedicated folders, connectors and approval screens won’t create much protection individually.
I would position the product around this promise:
Deploy a business task with defined permissions, predictable cost and a verifiable result.

The Upstash analogy works particularly well for the deployment experience: understandable configuration, quick activation, transparent usage and reliable operations. The customer-facing value is the completed business process.
The defensibility would need to accumulate in three places:
- Workflow competence. Tested handling of messy documents, duplicates, missing information, inconsistent records and interrupted execution. Each task package includes its checks, exceptions and recovery procedures.
- Operational trust. Customers can see what happened, why an action was permitted, what needs attention and how a failed job is resolved. They spend progressively less time supervising.
- Distribution and embedded configuration. A repeatable route to a specific buyer, plus useful policies, mappings and integrations that become established in their operations. Templates alone offer little protection.
The difficult strategic position is being squeezed between a bundled general-purpose agent and specialist software that already owns the customer’s workflow. A horizontal platform is a reasonable destination; a specific buyer and workflow family give it a stronger entry point. Self-service can remain the product ambition from day one.
My practical test: if someone can recreate the useful part by giving Tasklet a prompt and connecting two accounts, the differentiation is thin. If recreating it requires rebuilding validated exception handling, permissions, reconciliation and recovery—and customers repeatedly pay because you remove that burden—you have something stronger.
So I’d pursue it, with the research centred on which repeatable job we can deliver with materially less supervision, at a sustainable cost. That answer matters more to defensibility than the sandbox stack.

The deeper opportunity is to lower the minimum economically viable engagement between two businesses. An agent could make a small piece of another firm’s expertise, information or operational capability worth selling individually.
That is a stronger thesis than “businesses need managed agents”. But your examples contain several different businesses, and putting them into one app store won’t automatically make them work.
First, the valuable thing is what the provider can stand behind.
Consider three versions of the same study: a generic model-generated report, an analysis using a specialist firm’s proprietary information, and a report reviewed and issued by that firm for a stated purpose. They might look similar. Their commercial value differs substantially.
The purchase therefore needs to answer: what decision can this result support, and what responsibility does the provider accept? Accreditation on a profile is insufficient. The particular service needs to explain whether professional review and sign-off are included.
This suggests that the listing’s most important feature is its precise service definition. “Research agent” is difficult to buy. “A supplier assessment covering these questions, these sources and this review process” is something a business can evaluate.
Second, your examples have different sources of revenue.
Service	Likely commercial model to test	Main constraint
Proprietary information	Payment per result, usage allowance or subscription	Rights, freshness and access
Professionally reviewed work	Price per engagement, including review	Reviewer capacity and acceptance criteria
Supplier availability or quotation	Supplier-funded access, software subscription or revenue from the resulting order	Reliable operational information and conversion


A supplier stock check could be valuable while commanding almost no standalone price. The supplier might fund it because it helps sell stock. A signed specialist study might support a substantial engagement price, but still require expensive human attention.
One payment model across all three would obscure where the value actually sits.
Third, the supply-side proposition may be the best route into the market.
My hypothesis is that providers would participate if you help them sell work they currently struggle to serve profitably: requests too small for a conventional engagement, repetitive enquiries that consume staff time, or occasional buyers unwilling to purchase a full subscription.
That gives them a concrete reason to publish. You would be helping them package, distribute and operate a repeatable service while retaining control over their expertise and proprietary systems.
There is already evidence of this direction. Harvey lets firms share workflows and playbooks with clients, including workflows that produce drafts for professional review. That supports the delivery model, while establishing serious competition for provider relationships. Harvey Shared Spaces.
Fourth, a provider portal may precede a successful public marketplace.
A provider could first use the product to serve its existing customers. That supplies real demand, known counterparties and repeated transactions. Services could subsequently become discoverable to additional buyers.
This is a credible sequence because each provider gets useful capability before you achieve marketplace scale. The risk is that you build a collection of separate portals with little reason for customers to use the shared market. You would need evidence that buyers actually acquire services from additional providers.
Fifth, the platform’s defensibility depends on remaining useful after discovery.
Once a buyer finds a good professional or supplier, they can establish a direct relationship. Hosting and discovery alone leave you exposed to that.
You retain value if subsequent purchases remain easier through your platform: existing authorisations, comparable offers, consistent purchasing records, status visibility and effective remedies. Providers also need recurring demand and lower servicing costs.
Competition here includes established industry networks. SPS Commerce already connects trading partners and supports their data exchange; Thomson Reuters combines specialist content with AI products. Access to valuable supply is already a competitive asset. SPS Commerce, CoCounsel Legal.
Finally, the economics turn on exceptions and human work.
Illustratively, if the platform earns A$15 from a job and spends ten minutes resolving it at A$60 an hour, A$5 remains before compute, payments and other costs. Cheap inference does little to rescue that transaction.
The initial services therefore need repeatable inputs, clear eligibility, bounded outputs and predictable escalation. “Bounded” must make the service economical to operate as well as understandable to purchase.
I would test this through paid, repeated engagements from real providers—not through the number of agents launched. The strongest evidence would be providers profitably serving previously uneconomic requests, buyers returning, and some buyers purchasing from a second provider. That would establish both the managed-service business and the beginnings of a market.
