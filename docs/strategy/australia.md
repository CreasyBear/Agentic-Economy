# The nine hundred thousand firms nobody can buy from

*Companion to the thesis. September 2026. Sources: ABS, ATO, RBA, Treasury, Productivity Commission, Anthropic Economic Index.*

## 1. What Australia has

Australia has about one million employing businesses. 92.6 percent of them have fewer than twenty staff. Some 690,000 have fewer than five.

Most are specialists. A licensed surveyor. A fire engineer. A customs broker. A geotechnical lab. A planning consultant. An acoustics practice. A translator. Each holds a capability that somebody, somewhere, needs for one task and cannot do themselves.

This is an unusual economic shape. Australia is not a country of large firms with a fringe of small suppliers. It is a country of small firms, full stop. The capability is real, distributed and credentialed. What it does not have is a way to be bought.

## 2. Why the supply is stranded

A firm of three people cannot sell outside its postcode. Not because the work is local, but because the cost of finding and transacting with a customer exceeds the value of a small engagement.

To sell one task to a stranger, the firm must be found, quote, agree terms, contract, deliver, invoice and chase payment. That overhead is fixed. It does not shrink with the size of the job. A three thousand dollar engagement carries the same commercial machinery as a three hundred thousand dollar one.

So the firm does the rational thing. It sells few, large, local engagements to customers who already know it, and it absorbs the bench time in between.

Most of the capability in this economy is unbuyable. Not because nobody needs it. Because the cost of the first transaction is higher than the transaction.

## 3. What changes

Two things arrive together.

Software is starting to choose its own suppliers. And a service, once it is described precisely enough, becomes something a program can select and buy.

That means a firm's capability can be packaged as a bounded purchase. Fixed inputs. A firm price. A stated delivery condition. Accepted terms. A stated remedy. A record.

Once a service exists in that form, four things stop being the firm's problem. Being found. Being priced. Being paid. Being held to account. Those move to whoever operates the market.

The firm keeps doing the work. It loses the sales function, the receivables and the collections.

## 4. The offer, stated plainly

A service business today runs on a website that describes what it does and a phone number that rings.

The offer is a second front door. One that takes structured requests, returns firm prices, accepts delivery obligations and settles on delivery. The firm does not build it, maintain it, or need to understand it.

The end state is a network of Australian service firms that a program can transact with the way it transacts with anything else. Not a directory. Not lead generation. A set of businesses you can actually buy from.

## 5. Why Australia first

Not because the market is largest. Because the conditions for a first honest test are already here.

**One jurisdiction.** One goods and services tax, one company register, one consumer law that reaches digital services. The compliant path is built once and sold nationally, not rebuilt per customer.

**Legally gated data.** Land titles, corporate extracts, security registers, planning overlays, environmental records, energy dispatch. Each has a statutory character, a real price and no free substitute. These are the purchases that cannot complete without a seller.

**Work that models cannot absorb.** The usage data is useful here and only here. It says which work Australians already hand to machines. Insurance claims at 1.4 times the global rate. Office clerks at 1.3. Lawyers at 1.15. In Western Australia, compliance work at 1.44. That tells you which capabilities to make callable first. It does not tell you anyone will pay for them.

**A population that has settled how it pays.** Contactless is almost all in person card payments because it asked for nothing new. PayTo asked for new behaviour and has stalled for years. Any offer that asks a firm or its customer to learn a payment method, hold a new asset or change its accounts fails on behaviour before it fails on merit.

## 6. The sequencing problem

The honest position. Demand for machine purchasing is early. Almost nobody runs an agent that spends money with outside services today.

So the supply side cannot wait for that demand. It has to stand on its own.

It does, if you sell the right half. A firm that standardises its capability into a bounded, priced, deliverable purchase can sell it to any software-mediated buyer, whether a person approves the purchase or not. National reach, instant settlement and no collections are worth something on their own terms, today, with no agent involved.

The agent is the scale story, not the entry story. Building the supply as though agents were already buying is the mistake. Building it so it works the day they do is the position.

## 7. Inverse premortem: it is 2029 and it worked

Tens of thousands of Australian service firms are transactable. Buyers select on delivery record. Most of those firms have no other software revenue.

**In 2028** firms began listing without being recruited. That only happens if a listing pays, which means the 2027 buyers were real and repeat.

**In 2027** the repeat buyers were professional firms whose work needed records they could not get from a vendor they already had, and who could buy them without a procurement cycle. Business identity ran through Digital ID once it opened to the private sector.

**In 2026** one complete purchase ran end to end. A firm that had never sold to that buyer converted its capability into a callable, priced, deliverable purchase, and a stranger bought it and received an ordinary tax invoice.

That last link carries the most weight, and it is narrower than it looks. The proof is not that a payment settled. The proof is that a small service business successfully turned what it does into something buyable, and that somebody bought it. Everything after that is conditional on repeat buyers, which is the thing no document can assert.

## 8. How it fails

**Firms will not convert.** Packaging a service is work, and small firms do not do work that does not pay this quarter. If conversion requires a consultant, the market never forms.

**The work is too bespoke.** If every engagement needs negotiation, there is no standard purchase and no market. The available supply is then only the narrow band of work that can be bounded in advance.

**Buyers keep their incumbents.** The obvious needs already have vendors with accounts, integrations and switching costs. The offer only lands on work that has no incumbent at all.

**The bridge revenue never appears.** Demand for machine purchasing stays near zero, and the supply-side product cannot stand alone after all. This is the sequencing failure, and it is the one to watch.

**A platform takes the role.** A platform with enough buyers accepts liability for third party work in each jurisdiction and carries the sale itself. None has.

## 9. The claim

Australia has the supply and not the market. Roughly nine hundred thousand specialised firms hold capability that cannot be bought, because the cost of the first transaction is higher than the transaction.

Software is beginning to choose who does work. When it does, a service that has been made bounded, priced and deliverable becomes purchasable, and the firm that owns it stops needing a sales function to exist.

aecon builds the front door those firms have never had, and the standing that makes their work safe to buy.
