# Latent-capacity supply evidence: do operating companies sell what they know?

**Status:** Research complete  
**Decision affected:** Whether AE pursues the latent-capacity formation-layer wedge  
**Evidence cutoff:** 2026-07-22

## Executive verdict

**Verdict:** **Holders will sell, but only under restrictive conditions; the intermediary model is supported more strongly than unsupported DIY selling, but not yet at a population-level causal standard.**

- **OBSERVED:** Non-data operating companies have sustained external information products when the data is recurrent, difficult to reproduce, legally usable, pooled or broad enough to benchmark, and delivered as a decision product rather than a raw extract. Retail media/insight businesses, airline loyalty programs, bank-spend analytics, and telco mobility analytics are operating examples.
- **INFERRED:** The strongest pattern is not “upload dormant data and wait.” It is a dedicated product-and-go-to-market organization that normalizes inputs, protects contributors, measures buyer outcomes, and sells a workflow, score, audience, benchmark, or decision engine.
- **OBSERVED:** MIT CISR’s 2024 survey found top performers attributed 11% of revenue to data monetization versus 2% for bottom performers, and associated the gap with nearly twice-better monetization capabilities plus CEO leadership, value realization, and lifecycle measurement ([MIT CISR, 2024](https://cisr.mit.edu/publication/2024_1101_HighPerformanceDataMonetization_WixomBeathDuane)). This is association, not proof that intermediation causes success.
- **UNKNOWN:** No credible public study establishes the failure rate of *external selling by non-data companies* specifically. The often-repeated “80% fail” Gartner claim is narrower than commonly represented and should not be used as the base rate for this thesis.

The practical condition set is: **clear rights and consent; repeatable supply; incremental rather than core-conflicting value; a buyer decision worth changing; sufficient coverage or uniqueness; and a product owner with its own economics and sales motion.** AE’s plausible role is therefore formation and managed distribution—not a generic data marketplace.

## MIT CISR frame: improve, wrap, sell

**OBSERVED:** Wixom and Ross define three routes: (1) **improving** internal processes and decisions, (2) **wrapping** information around existing products and services, and (3) **selling** information offerings to existing or new markets ([MIT CISR, 2017](https://cisr.mit.edu/publication/how-monetize-your-data)). Improving avoids an external sales motion; wrapping can lift retention or price through an existing channel; selling creates a new information business and therefore demands the most new capability.

**OBSERVED:** MIT CISR’s 2018 survey of 315 executives identified five enterprise capabilities: data-asset curation, a data-factory platform, data-science talent, customer understanding, and acceptable data use ([MIT CISR survey report, 2019](https://cisr.mit.edu/publication/MIT_CISRwp437_DataMonetizationSurveyReport_Wixom)). Its public abstract does not publish a clean improve-versus-wrap-versus-sell success-rate table. **UNKNOWN:** Claims that MIT found “selling succeeds least” cannot be verified from the public report and are not asserted here.

**INFERRED:** The framework still narrows the thesis. Latent-capacity formation concerns the hardest route—selling—unless AE can first help a holder wrap an existing service. A wedge that begins with wrapping may have a better channel fit than asking an operator to become a standalone data vendor immediately.

## What actually blocks suppliers

1. **Acceptable use, rights and privacy.** **OBSERVED:** MIT names acceptable data use as one of five core capabilities, and FTC location-data orders demonstrate that violations can prohibit the sale itself. **INFERRED:** For personal or contributed data, this is the most absolute blocker: weak quality makes a poor product; absent lawful authority makes no product.
2. **Productization and buyer understanding.** **OBSERVED:** MIT’s five capabilities include customer understanding, while Wolters Kluwer gives expert solutions owners dedicated P&Ls and sales teams and kills products when market potential is absent ([MIT CISR, 2024](https://cisr.mit.edu/publication/2024_1101_HighPerformanceDataMonetization_WixomBeathDuane)). **INFERRED:** This is likely the most common commercial blocker, but no reviewed source ranks blockers by frequency.
3. **Quality, normalization and coverage.** **OBSERVED:** LexisNexis explicitly adds value through normalization, standardization, aggregation and linkage. FlightAware combines heterogeneous feeds. **INFERRED:** Dormant records are usually an input cost, not a finished product; sparse local supply will not support a benchmark without pooling.
4. **Sales-motion mismatch.** **OBSERVED:** CBA used Quantium; Singtel moved DataSpark into NCS; Wolters Kluwer dedicated sales teams. **INFERRED:** Existing operators can sell when the buyer overlaps their channel (retailer to suppliers), but unrelated buyers require a specialist channel.
5. **Channel conflict and cannibalization.** **INFERRED:** Holders rationally withhold data that reveals pricing, weakens information advantage, exposes customers, or helps competitors. Public case studies under-report rejected initiatives, so the magnitude is **UNKNOWN**.
6. **Unit economics and measurement.** **OBSERVED:** MIT distinguishes benefits from realized financial value and reports that high performers measure the data lifecycle and income-statement effect. **INFERRED:** Programs die when bespoke cleaning, compliance and consultative sales exceed repeat revenue.

### Make versus buy: transaction-cost reading

**INFERRED:** A holder should DIY when its data is inseparable from an existing customer relationship, buyers are already in its channel, and control is strategically important (Walmart, Kroger). It should partner when aggregation, neutral governance, specialized models, or a new sales channel dominate (CBA/Quantium, LexisNexis, FlightAware). An intermediary lowers search, contracting, normalization and compliance costs, but adds dependency and risks appropriating the supplier’s margin. This predicts a managed formation service for fragmented suppliers, not a passive exchange.

## Disconfirming evidence

- **OBSERVED:** Deere is frequently cited as data monetization, but the verified evidence is mainly product wrapping and customer data management—not sale of farmers’ data. Airline loyalty securitizations similarly monetize a contractual currency and partner channel more than “latent data.” These analogies overstate the thesis if treated as raw-data precedents.
- **OBSERVED:** Several celebrated internal ventures lost their independent form: LUCA was absorbed into Telefónica Tech and DataSpark into NCS. **INFERRED:** This may indicate channel consolidation rather than failure, but it weakens claims that every holder can support a standalone data company.
- **OBSERVED:** Location-data enforcement shows that a technically de-identified or aggregated proposition can still encounter unacceptable collection, consent or sensitive-use risk.
- **UNKNOWN:** Nearly every parent withholds product-level revenue. “Still marketed” proves persistence, not profitability. Survivor and publicity bias are severe; failed pilots rarely produce primary-source post-mortems.
- **INFERRED:** The best successes have structural advantages—national transaction scale, mandatory supplier relationships, network effects, or industry reciprocity. They are not representative of a small operating company with a modest spreadsheet.
- **INFERRED:** Agents may reduce discovery and delivery costs, but do not automatically resolve title, consent, quality, adverse selection, channel conflict or willingness to pay.

## Base rates

### Gartner claim audit

**OBSERVED:** The traceable Gartner prediction is commonly quoted as: **“By 2020, 80% of companies will fail to monetize their IoT data.”** A contemporaneous report attributes this to Gartner’s *100 Data and Analytics Predictions Through 2022* (document 3875982), but Gartner’s public document URL now redirects to its homepage, preventing verification of the full original wording ([Gartner document URL](https://www.gartner.com/en/documents/3875982); [contemporaneous quotation](https://www.gooddata.ai/blog/80-percent-of-companies-will-fail-to-monetize-iot-data-according-gartner/)). Therefore the claim is **OBSERVED only as a contemporaneous attributed quotation, not as directly read Gartner text**.

**Do not write:** “80% of data monetization programs fail through 2022.” That changes the population (IoT companies to all programs), the outcome, and the date. **UNKNOWN:** Gartner’s method, denominator and definition of “fail” were not publicly recoverable; it is a forecast, not an observed base rate.

### MIT base-rate evidence

**OBSERVED:** MIT CISR surveyed 349 senior leaders in 2024. Top performers (76) attributed 11% of revenues to data monetization; bottom performers (75) attributed 2%, with the difference statistically significant at p<.001. Top performers’ composite monetization capability was almost twice as strong ([MIT CISR, 2024](https://cisr.mit.edu/publication/2024_1101_HighPerformanceDataMonetization_WixomBeathDuane)). **OBSERVED:** The 2018 study surveyed 315 executives and found the five capabilities stated above ([MIT CISR, 2019](https://cisr.mit.edu/publication/MIT_CISRwp437_DataMonetizationSurveyReport_Wixom)). Neither public source gives an external-selling failure percentage.

**INFERRED:** The honest base-rate conclusion is dispersion, not 80% failure: returns concentrate among organizations with mature capabilities, while many report little revenue attribution. Selection, self-reporting and mixed monetization modes prevent translating this into “X% of holders successfully sell data.”

## Intermediary evidence: stronger, but conditional

**OBSERVED:** dunnhumby, Quantium, LexisNexis and FlightAware each perform several tasks the holder does not: combine sources, standardize data, define permissible uses, build decision products, and reach buyers. Kroger’s 84.51° and Walmart Data Ventures show the same functions can be captive when the holder has scale and channel.

**INFERRED:** This supports the *functions* of a formation layer more strongly than the proposition that an independent intermediary always wins. The decisive variable is not ownership; it is whether someone carries product management, governance, normalization, distribution and P&L responsibility. A third party is most defensible where supply is fragmented, neutrality or pooling increases value, and no holder can efficiently sell alone. It is less defensible where one retailer controls unique scaled data and its suppliers are already captive buyers.

**UNKNOWN:** There is no matched study comparing intermediary-assisted and DIY projects after controlling for holder scale and data quality. Evidence supports an existence proof and transaction-cost mechanism, not a universal success-rate advantage.

## What this kills or narrows

- Kills “companies already have sale-ready data.” Most have costly inputs requiring rights work, normalization and product design.
- Kills a passive catalogue or raw-data exchange as the primary wedge.
- Narrows initial supply away from personal/sensitive data unless authority and acceptable-use controls are exceptionally clear.
- Narrows targets to recurrent, decision-relevant observations with unique coverage or poolable value.
- Narrows AE’s role to formation plus managed distribution and measurable buyer outcomes; protocol alone is insufficient.
- Weakens the assumption that agents materially change supplier willingness. They lower transaction costs, not strategic conflict.
- Preserves a hypothesis: fragmented holders may benefit from a neutral specialist that returns reciprocal insight or revenue without exposing raw records.

## Open questions only fieldwork can answer

1. Will a holder sign a narrowly defined right to derive and resell an answer, rather than license raw data?
2. Which buyer decision changes often enough to cover onboarding, cleaning, privacy and sales cost?
3. What minimum source coverage makes the product credible, and can AE reach it before demand churns?
4. Does the holder prefer cash, reciprocal benchmarks, customer acquisition, or improved utilization?
5. Which internal executive owns title, acceptable use and channel-conflict approval?
6. Can one bounded product be delivered repeatedly without bespoke analyst work?
7. Will buyers accept an intermediary-derived result and its evidence, or demand the holder’s direct warranty?
8. Does an agent-mediated per-answer price improve economics versus an existing subscription or consulting sale?
